SATELLITE ORBIT DATA EXPLORATION

Satellite
Distributions Around Earth

Satellites are not distributed evenly around Earth. Their position depends on mission requirements, orbital constraints, ownership, and strategic priorities. This visualization explores how satellites are organized across Low Earth Orbit, Medium Earth Orbit, and Geostationary Orbit, highlighting the relationship between altitude, density, and geopolitical or commercial presence in space.

ORBITAL STRUCTURE

A Layered View of Earth Orbits

Satellites are grouped into Low, Medium and Geostationary Earth Orbits using average altitude derived from apogee and perigee.
Selecting an owner reveals how its satellites are distributed across these orbital regions.

LOW EARTH ORBIT

LEO Satellite Layers

Low Earth Orbit (LEO) contains the vast majority of active satellites. This region is heavily populated by communication mega-constellations, Earth observation systems and scientific missions operating below 2,000 km altitude.

The visualization divides LEO into altitude segments and highlights the dominant satellite owner in each layer.

MEDIUM EARTH ORBIT

MEO Ownership Bands

Medium Earth Orbit (MEO) is far less crowded than LEO and is mainly used for navigation and positioning systems such as GPS, Galileo, GLONASS and BeiDou.

The visualization divides MEO into altitude segments and highlights the dominant satellite owner within each orbital layer between 2,000 km and geostationary orbit.

GEOSTATIONARY ORBIT

GEO Belt View

GEO satellites remain fixed over longitude, making ownership patterns visible along the equatorial belt.

This use the current CelesTrak GEO snapshot, so they can differ from the altitude-based overview above, which is derived from CelesTrak SATCAT.

CONTEXT

Why This Plot Matters

Space is often described as a global commons: no country can claim sovereignty over an orbital region in the same way it can claim territory on Earth. However, orbital space is not unlimited in practice. Satellites require specific altitudes, trajectories, and separation from other objects to operate safely.

This means that access to useful orbital regions can become constrained. When many satellites are placed in similar orbital bands, those regions become more crowded, increasing collision risks and making future deployments more complex. In this sense, orbital occupation can create a practical form of control: even without legal ownership, actors with large satellite fleets can shape who is able to use certain parts of near-Earth space.

This plot is therefore important because it shows more than where satellites are located. It reveals how orbital presence can translate into strategic advantage, and why the distribution of satellites across LEO, MEO, and GEO matters for the future accessibility of space.
